# Alkynes — Acetylide SN2 and Double E2

> Source feed: `raw/ch 9.pdf` (Wade, Chapter 9). This note only records links to the four core mechanisms. See [index](index.md).

## Overview

Alkynes are connected to substitution and elimination in two ways. An **acetylide ion** (a strong base and powerful nucleophile) displaces a halide by SN2 to lengthen a carbon chain. An alkyne can also be made by eliminating two molecules of HX from a dihalide (two E2 steps).

## Kinetics & Rate Law

No new rate laws. The acetylide alkylation is [SN2](sn2.md), so `Rate = k[substrate][nucleophile]`. The dehydrohalogenation steps are [E2](e2.md), `Rate = k[substrate][base]`.

## Step-by-Step Mechanism

1. **Make the acetylide:** a terminal alkyne reacts with NaNH₂. Terminal alkynes have pKa ≈ 25, and NH₃ has pKa ≈ 36, so amide is strong enough to deprotonate them. Acidity increases in the order sp³ < sp² < sp.
2. **Alkylate it (SN2):** R–C≡C⁻ + R′–X → R–C≡C–R′ + X⁻. Example: sodium acetylide + 1-bromobutane → hex-1-yne (75%).
3. **Double dehydrohalogenation:** a vicinal or geminal dihalide loses HX to form a vinyl halide (fast), then a second HX under strongly basic conditions to form the alkyne (slow).

## Stereochemistry

No stereocenter is created in these steps. The SN2 step still needs back-side approach, so a hindered halide fails (see Controlling Factors).

## Controlling Factors

- The halide R′–X must be methyl or primary, with no bulky groups near the reaction center. It can also be a primary tosylate.
- If the back-side approach is hindered (for example isopropyl bromide), the acetylide acts as a **base** and gives **E2** elimination instead: butynide + isopropyl bromide → but-1-yne + propene.
- Fused KOH forms internal alkynes. NaNH₂ forms terminal alkynes (severe conditions).
